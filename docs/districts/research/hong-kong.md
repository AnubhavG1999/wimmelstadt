HONG KONG district catalogue (facts; items marked [unverified] come from my background knowledge, not a 2026 source)

1. CHARACTER
From above, Hong Kong reads as extreme vertical density: dense clusters of slim, tall white, grey and glass towers, many with flat roofs carrying water tanks, rooftop huts and antenna masts, packed against steep green hills and cut by a dark-blue harbour. Colour comes from the red taxis, red-and-cream or green double-decker trams, neon and bamboo-scaffolded construction, and green hillsides behind grey estates. Streets are tight grids on reclaimed flat land (Central, Wan Chai, Tsim Sha Tsui, Mong Kok) and contour-hugging switchbacks up the slopes (Mid-Levels, Peak). Overhead, the sky is full of signs jutting sideways over the street, and the skyline is stepped: tallest at the harbour's edge, rising to the Peak.

2. GROUND
- Hong Kong Island (north shore): a narrow strip of reclaimed land along the water (Central, Wan Chai, Causeway Bay) with a steep green ridge immediately behind. Victoria Peak is about 552 m. Mid-Levels is stepped terraces of towers. Roads zigzag; the Central-Mid-Levels escalator climbs the slope. Parks: Hong Kong Park, Zoological and Botanical Gardens, Victoria Park (large rectangle in Causeway Bay).
- Kowloon: flatter and a more regular grid, bounded by the Kowloon Hills to the north (Lion Rock). The Tsim Sha Tsui waterfront promenade faces the island; Kowloon Park sits just inland. Kai Tak, the old airport, is now a flat redeveloped runway peninsula with a cruise terminal. Mong Kok is the densest grid.
- Harbour: roughly 1 to 3 km wide, narrowest around Central to Tsim Sha Tsui. Tunnels pass beneath it (Cross-Harbour, Eastern, Western).
- Contrast: the island is a steep, narrow, vertical, mostly white and glass business face. Kowloon is flatter, more regular, with lower, older, signage-heavy blocks and wide boulevards (Nathan Road, the "Golden Mile").

3. LANDMARKS (most iconic first)
1. Victoria Harbour skyline from Tsim Sha Tsui promenade: the stepped line of towers on the island shore, seen across the water.
2. Bank of China Tower (Central, I. M. Pei): silver-black, a stack of triangular prisms rising in stepped diagonals, two antenna masts on top, about 367 m. Sits mid-harbour-front behind Statue Square and Chater Garden, just east of HSBC. Roads run around it and a flyover passes alongside.
3. HSBC Main Building (Central): grey-silver, with exposed steel truss "coat hanger" structure, flat roof, stepped sides, an open ground-floor plaza underneath that pedestrians cross. Two bronze lions at the entrance. Faces Statue Square, close to the Bank of China Tower. A pedestrian plaza runs under it; roads skirt it.
4. Peak Tower and the Peak Tram (Victoria Peak): Peak Tower is a curved, wok-shaped building with a bowl-shaped roof, white and grey, on the summit's shoulder. The funicular tram climbs steeply behind Central to the Peak from Garden Road; its upper terminus meets the tower. The tram crosses roads on bridges and passes under/over some. Upgraded in 2022; cars hold about 210 passengers.
5. International Commerce Centre (ICC, West Kowloon): tall, slim glass tower with a flat top, about 484 m, tallest in Hong Kong, near Kowloon Station. It sits on the Kowloon waterfront side, opposite Central, across the harbour.
6. Two International Finance Centre (2IFC, Central): tall, slim, pale glass tower with a rounded-notched crown, beside the Central ferry piers and airport express station. Waterfront, with road flyovers around the base.
7. Star Ferry piers and green-and-white ferries (Central and Tsim Sha Tsui): small white-and-green double-deck boats, with green-and-white pier buildings. They cross between Central/Wan Chai and Tsim Sha Tsui. The Tsim Sha Tsui pier is by the clock tower.
8. Tsim Sha Tsui Clock Tower: a slim red-brick-and-granite tower, about 44 m, a remnant of the old Kowloon-Canton railway terminus, on the waterfront next to the Cultural Centre.
9. Tian Tan Buddha (Ngong Ping, Lantau): large bronze seated figure on a stepped granite platform, beside Po Lin Monastery. Outside Victoria Harbour proper; optional outlier.
10. Hong Kong Cultural Centre (Tsim Sha Tsui): a long, low, curved, windowless, pale-pink/beige block with a sweeping slanted roof, right on the waterfront by the Clock Tower and Star Ferry.
11. Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre (Wan Chai): a bird-wing-shaped glass-and-white roof on reclaimed land jutting into the harbour, with the Golden Bauhinia monument outside. [unverified detail: bauhinia is a gold flower statue on a granite base]
12. The Peninsula Hotel (Tsim Sha Tsui): a cream-white, wing-shaped colonial-style hotel on Salisbury Road with a big helipad on top, near Nathan Road and the harbour.
Also useful: Central Plaza (Wan Chai) triangular tower with a lit mast; Ngong Ping 360 cable car; Man Mo Temple (Sheung Wan) with hanging incense coils; Chungking Mansions (Nathan Road).

Roads: the Cross-Harbour Tunnel, Eastern Harbour Crossing and Western Harbour Crossing go under the water. Connaught Road Central and Harcourt Road are the main island harbour-front roads, with flyovers passing close to IFC and the Bank of China Tower. Garden Road climbs to the Peak. Nathan Road is the main Kowloon artery.

5. STREET LIFE
- Taxis: red-and-silver for the urban area (Hong Kong Island, Kowloon, and most of the New Territories reach), green-and-white for the New Territories, and blue-and-silver for Lantau. Fleet taxis (new colours from 2025) exist, so a bit of colour variety is accurate. Taxis have a bright roof lamp sign.
- Trams: narrow double-decker "Ding Ding" trams on the island's north shore, running east-west on Des Voeux Road and Hennessy Road; usually covered in full-body ads, otherwise dark green with a cream/yellow band. Open upper deck.
- Buses: red-and-cream or red-white double-deckers (KMB, Citybus yellow/blue, NWFB), plus blue or orange for some routes.
- Minibuses: red-roofed and green-roofed, white body; 16 seats.
- Ferries: Star Ferry green-and-white; other ferries are white with colours.
- Junks: a few red-sail tourist junks (Aqua Luna) cross the harbour.
- Street furniture: bamboo scaffolding on building sides, neon signs sticking out, railings, air-con units, and dai pai dong stalls with plastic stools. Pedestrian footbridges and covered walkways.
- Signs: traditional Chinese characters (Cantonese), plus English; vertical sign boards; some bilingual.
- People: office workers in Central; elderly doing tai chi; domestic helpers gathering on Sundays at Central; mahjong; shoppers at markets (Ladies' Market, Temple Street).

7. WATCH OUT
- Neon signs are being removed: the Buildings Department calls them a safety problem and LED replacements are common; fewer than 500 remain by one estimate. In 2026, reports say a bridal wear shop took down its famous neon on government orders and a decades-old bakery removed its sign for building codes [dates not confirmed]. Neon survivors are preserved at M+ museum and by the Tetra Neon Exchange. Use neon as "endangered", not guaranteed.
- Restaurant closures are widespread in 2026 (see section 4), driven by an F&B slump and mainland travel by locals.
- Sensitivities: political topics (2019 protests, the National Security Law), the 1997 handover, and the Chinese flag/Bauhinia flag symbols. Keep the toy city apolitical. Use "Hong Kong" and "Kowloon" neutrally; avoid mock-ups of protest scenes. Be careful with parody names of real brands (use clear pun, no logos). Avoid religious sites as props if their treatment would be disrespectful (Man Mo Temple, Po Lin).
- Taxi colour: confirmed by general sources that there are three colours; exact region mapping is from my background knowledge.
